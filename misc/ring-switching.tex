\begin{center}
Turning $64$ evaluation claims over $\F_{2^{192}}$ into one opening of a commitment whose values are $64$ bits wide.
\end{center}

\section*{Setting}

Two fields, one inside the other:
\[
\F_{2^{64}}=\F_2[X]/(X^{64}+X^4+X^3+X+1),
\qquad
\F_{2^{192}}=\F_{2^{64}}[Y]/(Y^3+Y+1).
\]
As $\F_2$-vector spaces, $\F_{2^{64}}$ has basis $X^0,\dots,X^{63}$, and $\F_{2^{192}}$ has the $192$ products
\[
b_w=X^iY^j\in\F_{2^{192}},\qquad w=64j+i,\quad 0\le i<64,\quad 0\le j<3.
\]

The witness is $64$ bit-valued multilinears $P_0,\dots,P_{63}:\{0,1\}^m\to\{0,1\}$. We commit to them packed, one $\F_{2^{64}}$ element per point:
\[
P:\{0,1\}^m\to\F_{2^{64}},\qquad P(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{63}P_i(z)\,X^i.
\]
The commitment scheme proves statements of one shape. Given a target $T\in\F_{2^{192}}$ and a transparent weight $W:\{0,1\}^m\to\F_{2^{192}}$, one that the verifier can evaluate for itself, an opening proves
\[
\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}W(z)\,P(z)=T.
\]
The weight is $\F_{2^{192}}$-valued, the committed values are $\F_{2^{64}}$-valued, and the sum is taken in $\F_{2^{192}}$.

What arrives is not of that shape. It is $64$ claimed values $\alpha_i\in\F_{2^{192}}$, one per bit polynomial, at a point $r\in\F_{2^{192}}^m$ they all share. An honest prover sends
\[
\alpha_i=P_i(r)=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}\eq(r,z)\,P_i(z),\qquad 0\le i<64,
\]
where $\eq:\F_{2^{192}}^m\times\F_{2^{192}}^m\to\F_{2^{192}}$ is the equality indicator
\[
\eq(u,v)=\prod_{n=1}^m\bigl(u_nv_n+(1+u_n)(1+v_n)\bigr),
\]
and $P_i(r)$ denotes the multilinear extension of $P_i$ evaluated at $r$, as it does for every multilinear below.
Ring switching~\cite{DP26} turns those $64$ claims into a single statement the opening can prove.

\section*{The reduction}

Fix an $\F_2$-linear map $\Phi:\F_{2^{192}}\to\F_{2^{192}}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}\Phi\bigl(\eq(r,z)\bigr)\,P(z)
&=\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}P_i(z)\,\Phi\bigl(\eq(r,z)\bigr)\\
&=\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\,\Phi\Bigl(\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}P_i(z)\,\eq(r,z)\Bigr)
=\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\,\Phi(\alpha_i).
\end{split}
\label{eq:complete}
\end{equation}
The reduction is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item once the $\alpha_i$ are fixed, the verifier draws $\Phi$ at random;
\item it computes the target $T=\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\Phi(\alpha_i)$;
\item the commitment scheme opens $P$ against the weight $W:\{0,1\}^m\to\F_{2^{192}}$, $W(z)=\Phi\bigl(\eq(r,z)\bigr)$, with target $T$; if that opening accepts, the verifier accepts the $64$ claims.
\end{enumerate}
Everything now turns on which $\Phi$ we can afford to draw.

\section*{Which maps are sound}

\paragraph{The error, transposed.} Suppose the $\alpha_i$ are wrong. The errors $\delta_i=\alpha_i-P_i(r)\in\F_{2^{192}}$, for $0\le i<64$, are not all zero, and they are fixed before $\Phi$ is drawn. By \eqref{eq:complete} the target the verifier computes differs from the honest one by $\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\Phi(\delta_i)$, so the opening succeeds only if
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\,\Phi(\delta_i)=0.
\]
Expand each error in the $\F_2$-basis of $\F_{2^{192}}$, say $\delta_i=\sum_{w=0}^{191}\delta_{i,w}b_w$ with $\delta_{i,w}\in\F_2$, and exchange the two sums:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\,\Phi(\delta_i)
=\sum_{i=0}^{63}X^i\sum_{w=0}^{191}\delta_{i,w}\,\Phi(b_w)
=\sum_{w=0}^{191}\Phi(b_w)\,\Delta_w,
\qquad
\Delta_w:=\sum_{i=0}^{63}\delta_{i,w}X^i\in\F_{2^{64}}.
\label{eq:transpose}
\end{equation}
The $X^i$ are an $\F_2$-basis of $\F_{2^{64}}$, so some $\Delta_w\neq0$. The check passes only if $\sum_{w=0}^{191}\Phi(b_w)\,\Delta_w=0$. No fixed $\Phi$ rules that out, so the draw must make it unlikely.

\paragraph{Linear maps are Frobenius sums.} Squaring is additive in characteristic $2$, so
\[
\Phi(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{191}c_k\,a^{2^k},\qquad a\in\F_{2^{192}},\quad c_k\in\F_{2^{192}},
\]
is $\F_2$-linear for any choice of coefficients. Every $\F_2$-linear map $\F_{2^{192}}\to\F_{2^{192}}$ arises this way, and exactly once. Distinct tuples give distinct maps: their difference would be a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $2^{191}$ with $2^{192}$ roots. There are $|\F_{2^{192}}|^{192}$ tuples and as many maps. Call $S=\{k:c_k\neq0\}\subseteq\{0,\dots,191\}$ the \emph{support} of $\Phi$. Substituting into the check above,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{w=0}^{191}\Phi(b_w)\,\Delta_w=\sum_{k\in S}c_k\,V_k,
\qquad
V_k:=\sum_{w=0}^{191}b_w^{2^k}\,\Delta_w\in\F_{2^{192}}.
\label{eq:pairing}
\end{equation}
The $V_k$ depend only on the error, the $c_k$ only on the randomness. So we need the map $\F_{2^{64}}^{192}\to\F_{2^{192}}^{|S|}$, $\Delta\mapsto(V_k)_{k\in S}$, to be injective: a nonzero error has to leave some $V_k\neq0$ for the coefficients to act on.

\paragraph{Sixty-four terms.} That map is $\F_{2^{64}}$-linear, since the $\Delta_w$ enter linearly and the Frobenius touches only the constants $b_w$. It sends $\F_{2^{64}}^{192}$, of $\F_{2^{64}}$-dimension $192$, into $\F_{2^{192}}^{|S|}$, of $\F_{2^{64}}$-dimension $3|S|$, so injectivity needs $|S|\ge64$. Take $S=\{0,\dots,63\}$ and suppose $V_k=0$ for all $k<64$. Two steps force $\Delta=0$.

First, split $V_k$ over $\F_{2^{64}}$. Write $w=64j+i$ and $Y_k:=Y^{2^k}$, so that $b_w^{2^k}=X^{i2^k}Y_k^{\,j}$ and
\[
V_k=\sum_{j=0}^{2}Y_k^{\,j}\,R_{j,k},
\qquad
R_{j,k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{63}\Delta_{64j+i}\,X^{i2^k}\in\F_{2^{64}},\qquad 0\le j<3.
\]
Now $Y_k\notin\F_{2^{64}}$. Squaring is a bijection of $\F_{2^{64}}$ onto itself, so $Y^{2^k}\in\F_{2^{64}}$ would force $Y\in\F_{2^{64}}$, whence $\F_{2^{192}}=\F_{2^{64}}[Y]=\F_{2^{64}}$. Since $[\F_{2^{192}}:\F_{2^{64}}]=3$ is prime, any element outside $\F_{2^{64}}$ generates $\F_{2^{192}}$ over $\F_{2^{64}}$, so $\{1,Y_k,Y_k^2\}$ is an $\F_{2^{64}}$-basis of $\F_{2^{192}}$, and $V_k=0$ forces $R_{0,k}=R_{1,k}=R_{2,k}=0$.

Second, read the $64$ equations $R_{j,k}=0$, at fixed $j$, as a single polynomial. Put
\[
D_j(U)=\sum_{i=0}^{63}\Delta_{64j+i}\,U^i\in\F_{2^{64}}[U],
\qquad\text{so that}\qquad
R_{j,k}=D_j\bigl(X^{2^k}\bigr).
\]
Then $D_j$ has degree at most $63$ and vanishes at the $64$ points $X,X^2,X^4,\dots,X^{2^{63}}$. Those are distinct, because $X$ has degree $64$ over $\F_2$ and so has $64$ distinct conjugates. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most $63$ cannot have $64$ roots, so $D_j=0$ and every $\Delta_{64j+i}$ vanishes. A nonzero error therefore leaves some $V_k\neq0$.

The count is tight on both sides: $64$ terms give $64$ equations $V_k=0$ over $\F_{2^{192}}$, which is $192$ equations over $\F_{2^{64}}$, against exactly $192$ unknowns $\Delta_w\in\F_{2^{64}}$.

\section*{The map we draw}

We need a random $\Phi$ of support $\{0,\dots,63\}$, cheap to apply. Sixty-four independent coefficients would need $64$ challenges and $64$ multiplications per application, and a false claim would then survive with probability $2^{-192}$. Six challenges are enough, at less than $2^{-160}$.

Draw $\gamma_0,\dots,\gamma_5\leftarrow\F_{2^{192}}$ once the $\alpha_i$ are fixed, and compose six two-term maps: for an input $a\in\F_{2^{192}}$, put $a_0=a$ and
\begin{equation}
a_{p+1}=a_p+\gamma_p\,a_p^{2^{2^{5-p}}}\in\F_{2^{192}}\quad(0\le p<6),
\qquad
\Phi(a)=a_6.
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
Each stage is $\F_2$-linear, so $\Phi$ is. Write $k=\sum_{p=0}^{5}k_p\,2^{5-p}$ for the binary digits of $k$. The composition expands to
\begin{equation}
\Phi(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{63}c_k\,a^{2^k},
\qquad
c_k=\prod_{p\,:\,k_p=1}\gamma_p^{\,2^{(k\bmod 2^{5-p})}}.
\label{eq:ck}
\end{equation}
Stage $p$ fires when $k_p=1$ and contributes a factor $\gamma_p$, which every later stage that fires raises to its own Frobenius power; those shifts sum to $k\bmod 2^{5-p}$. Each $k$ from $0$ to $63$ occurs exactly once, so the $c_k$ are $64$ distinct monomials in $\gamma_0,\dots,\gamma_5$, one for every Frobenius power the previous section requires.

\paragraph{Soundness.} Fix a nonzero error before the challenges are drawn. The previous section gives some $V_k\neq0$, and distinct monomials cannot cancel, so
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{63}c_k\,V_k
\]
is a nonzero polynomial in $\gamma_0,\dots,\gamma_5$. Its total degree is at most the largest degree of a $c_k$, reached at $k=63$ where every bit is set:
\[
2^{31}+2^{15}+2^7+2^3+2+1<2^{32},
\]
so by Schwartz--Zippel over $\F_{2^{192}}$ the check passes with probability less than $2^{32}/2^{192}=2^{-160}$, before the opening's own soundness error.

\section*{Evaluating the weight}

An opening reduces $\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}W(z)\,P(z)=T$ to a claim about $P$ at a random point $r'\in\F_{2^{192}}^m$, and the verifier must evaluate the multilinear extension of $W$ at $r'$, written $W(r')$, by itself. Two facts turn that into a short formula.

\emph{Fact 1 ($\eq$ in characteristic $2$).} In each factor the two $u_nv_n$ terms cancel, identically in $u$ and $v$:
\[
\eq(u,v)=\prod_{n=1}^m(1+u_n+v_n).
\]

\emph{Fact 2 (Frobenius passes through $\eq$ on the cube).} Squaring respects sums and products and fixes bits, so for $z\in\{0,1\}^m$, by Fact~1,
\[
\eq(r,z)^{2^k}=\prod_{n=1}^m(1+r_n+z_n)^{2^k}=\prod_{n=1}^m\bigl(1+r_n^{2^k}+z_n\bigr)=\eq\bigl(r^{2^k},z\bigr),
\qquad r^{2^k}:=\bigl(r_1^{2^k},\dots,r_m^{2^k}\bigr).
\]

Expand $W$ at $r'$ with \eqref{eq:ck} and Fact~2:
\[
W(r')=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}W(z)\,\eq(r',z)
=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}\Phi\bigl(\eq(r,z)\bigr)\,\eq(r',z)
=\sum_{k=0}^{63}c_k\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}\eq\bigl(r^{2^k},z\bigr)\,\eq(r',z).
\]
The inner sum is $\eq(r^{2^k},r')$, since $\eq(u,\cdot\,)$ is its own multilinear extension for every $u$. By Fact~1 again,
\[
\boxed{\ \
W(r') \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{63}\, c_k \prod_{n=1}^m
\bigl( 1 + r_n^{2^k} + r'_n \bigr).
\ \ }
\]

\section*{Cost}

One application of $\Phi$ takes $63$ squarings and $6$ multiplications: the Frobenius ladder climbs $32+16+8+4+2+1$ steps, and each stage multiplies once by its challenge. The same recurrence run on the coefficient table gives the $c_k$ of \eqref{eq:ck}: stage $p$ raises its $2^p$ entries to the $2^{2^{5-p}}$ power and multiplies each by $\gamma_p$; the raising is $32$ squarings whatever $p$ is.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
& squarings & multiplications\\[2pt]
\hline
one application of $\Phi$ & $63$ & $6$\\
the target $T$, $64$ applications & $4032$ & $384$\\
the coefficients $c_k$, once & $192$ & $63$\\
the weight $W(r')$ & $63m$ & $64m$\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Everything above is in $\F_{2^{192}}$. The $64$ products $X^i\,\Phi(\alpha_i)$ in the target are left out: $X^i$ lies in $\F_{2^{64}}$, so they are cheaper, and stepping $X^i$ to $X^{i+1}$ is a shift.
